Prologue · The Archivist's Confession

Sixty Cups on One Side of the Counter

For over a year now, I have kept a ledger. Every specialty filter coffee I drink — sixty entries, thirty-four venues, four cities, thirteen Geshas — gets decoded through the same discipline: taste it hot, taste it mid-cool, taste it cool. Write down what the cup actually said, not what the bean card promised. My palate has a documented identity by now. I know what I respond to: malic acid brightness, the stone-fruit register. Linalool florality — jasmine, orange blossom. Washed process for genetic transparency. I even know where my ceiling is, because one coffee showed it to me and I wrote it down.

But in all sixty entries, I have only ever been the person the coffee was served to. The one who receives, evaluates, archives. The barista performs; I decode. That has been the arrangement, cup after cup, city after city.

On the tenth of July, the arrangement inverted. Someone handed me a score sheet, sat me at a judge's station with a gold spoon and a matte black tray, and a barista preparing for the national championship performed for me — and then waited for my verdict.

This is the story of that evening. And like every good cup I've ever logged, it revealed itself in phases.

I · Hot Phase — First Contact

The Call That Changed the Evening

It started with a phone call I nearly didn't take.

I was in the middle of a cupping masterclass — the kind of session where the phone stays face-down and attention stays on the bowl. When Alex called, I let it ring. Sensory work doesn't pause for notifications. When I called him back later, what he said made me recalibrate the entire rest of my day.

He had been invited to Homeground Coffee Roasters in Bukit Timah to serve as a mock judge for one of their baristas preparing for competition. After sitting through yesterday's session himself, Alex decided I would be better suited for the role. He wanted to bring me in and introduce me to the team.

I didn't think twice. I cancelled my 6:30pm gym session and moved it to Saturday morning. Some invitations don't come with a rain check option.

The logistics assembled themselves quickly: meet Alex at Oaks Coffee Roaster in Bukit Timah at 4:30pm, coffee and briefing until Oaks closes at 5:30pm, one train stop to 6th Avenue, a short walk, arrive at Homeground by 6pm for a 6:30 start.

II · The Farm That Followed Me

El Diviso, Twice in One Day

Here is something I could not have scripted.

The day before, at Oaks, I had logged Entry #059 of my archive: Finca El Diviso by Nestor Lasso — a Colombia Aji Bourbon, Thermal Shock Anaerobic Natural. It was the coffee that confirmed, in writing, where my intensity ceiling lives. The fermentation-forward wildness, the thermal shock aggression — I rated it honestly: definitely not repeat. It wasn't a bad coffee. It was a coffee that exceeded the boundary of what my palate calls pleasure. Every archive needs its outer markers, and El Diviso drew mine.

Today, at Oaks again before the session, El Diviso was still on the menu — this time I chose it knowingly, almost as a salute. Berries, a bit wild, punchy exactly as anticipated. I drank it the way you re-read a difficult book: not for pleasure, but for confirmation.

Then, ninety minutes later, I sat down at the judge's station at Homeground, looked at the score sheet, and read the blend composition for Ho Ming's national championship bid:

Component A
50% Panama, Iris Estate — Gesha, Nitrogen Maceration
Component B
50% Colombia, El Diviso — Sidra, Anaerobic Yeast & Thermal Shock

El Diviso. Again. The same farm — Nestor Lasso's celebrated operation in Huila — that had drawn my archive's outer boundary was now standing at the center of a championship blend, this time as a Sidra varietal, tamed and balanced against the most refined thing in coffee: an Iris Estate Panama Gesha.

The same processing philosophy that had overwhelmed me in a solo cup was here being deployed as a structural element — the Sidra's fermented depth providing body and complexity underneath the Gesha's florals, rather than dominating the frame. It's the difference between a soloist playing too loud and the same instrument seated correctly in an orchestra.

One farm. Two days. Two completely different answers to the same question: how much intensity can a cup carry before it stops being coffee and starts being an argument?

III · The Room Elysia Opened

A Champion Turns On the Lights

When we arrived at Homeground, it was technically closed. But inside, controlled urgency: photos being taken, a camera positioned near the barista station, equipment aligned with the precision of people rehearsing something that matters.

The person who saw us through the glass and opened the door was Elysia Tan — co-founder and Director of Homeground Coffee Roasters. Her résumé requires its own paragraph: Singapore National Brewers Cup Champion in 2019, 2022, and 2024. World Brewers Cup 2022 Second Runner-Up — third best hand-brewer on the planet that year. One of the most decorated coffee professionals Singapore has ever produced.

She led with none of it. She turned on the lights and the air-conditioning, gestured us to seats in a café that wasn't open, and said they'd start at 6:30 sharp. Warm, direct, completely unadorned by her own trophy cabinet. There is a particular kind of confidence that no longer needs to announce itself, and it was standing at the door holding it open.

Then she mentioned there was still one more judging spot available — Alex could stay. He hesitated; yesterday's competition espresso had kept him awake until 2am. I looked at him and delivered the most Singaporean sports psychology available: "Just stay lah. You don't have to finish the drinks. Just taste them." He stayed.

The Singapore National Coffee Championship 2026 runs 15–17 July at Sands Expo, staged within SIGEP Asia. Sixty-seven competitors. Four categories. The winner of the Barista Championship earns the right to represent Singapore at the World Coffee Championships 2027.

He came into the room composed on the surface, but I could read the quiet rehearsal running behind his eyes — cue-checking, sequence-anchoring, the breathing of a man managing his own adrenaline. I recognized it because I've watched dozens of baristas work, and the great ones all carry pressure the same way: visibly, but with the weight distributed.

The full technical architecture of his presentation, from the score sheet:

Milk Build
70% Cow · 20% Soy · 10% Coconut — engineered for structure, sweetness, and a spark of fruitiness. The soy fraction builds protein scaffold; the coconut fraction contributes medium-chain lipids for a silkier emulsion.
Espresso
New Extraction Style · TDS 8% · Extraction 24% · Yield Ratio 1:2.9. A deliberately lean, high-extraction shot — pulling deeper into the sugar-browning compounds while keeping concentration soft enough for clarity.
Signature
4 shots espresso · 40g Jasmine Shrubs · 1g Brown Rice Vinegar · 5g Simple Syrup · 100g Lacto-Fermented Passionfruit (100g fruit : 1g salt, 0.2g clarified agar powder, 7-day fermentation) · Waved & sous vide at 70°C, 1 hour.
Rubric
1st sip — evaluate tactile. 2nd & 3rd sip — evaluate flavors. The judging structure itself reveals the engineering priority: mouthfeel first, flavor confirmation second.

A note on that espresso spec, because it deserves one. A 1:2.9 yield ratio at 24% extraction is a statement of philosophy. My own archive has repeatedly documented what tight ratios (1:13–1:14 in filter terms) do wrong — they amplify polyphenolic bitterness at cool temperatures and suppress sucrose expression. Ho Ming's approach runs the opposite direction: extract deep but dilute generously, chasing the sweetness compounds while diluting past the bitterness threshold. It's the espresso equivalent of my own 1:15–1:17 filter preference. I recognized the logic before he ever explained it, because I had archived its mirror image.

V · The Thesis

High Definition — The Concept That Was Already Mine

When Ho Ming began his presentation, I settled in the way I settle in for any new coffee: without preconceptions, waiting for the cup to tell me what it is.

His opening concept was called "High Definition." And as he laid it out, something strange happened: I heard my own archive speaking back to me.

Ho Ming's Opening Thesis · SNCC 2026
"What really matters is whether the final cup delivers the clarity it was always meant to."
From farmer to roaster to barista — if the jasmine isn't there, if the floral note doesn't arrive, then even an expensive, technically impeccable coffee has failed its purpose. Clarity is not a byproduct. It is the point.

Sixty entries ago, I began my archive with a founding principle I've since printed at the top of my palate identity: Clarity over Complexity. It's the sentence that explains why I chase washed-process Geshas, why I distrust fermentation that shouts, why my highest-rated cups are the ones where I can taste the varietal's genetic signature without interference. I arrived at it empirically — cup after cup, verdict after verdict.

Ho Ming arrived at the same place from the other side of the counter — through roasting curves, extraction math, and years of watching drinks succeed or fail in front of customers. Two completely different methodologies. One identical conclusion.

The Archivist · 60 Entries
"Clarity over Complexity."
Arrived at through sensory documentation — three thermal phases per cup, thirty-four venues, thirteen Geshas. The washed process preferred because it shows the bean's genetics without editorial.
The Competitor · SNCC 2026
"High Definition."
Arrived at through production — roasting, blending, extraction engineering. The conviction that a cup's job is to transmit what the farm created, at full resolution, with no signal loss.

This is why the evening mattered more than its three drinks. Somewhere between a farm in Huila and a judging table in Bukit Timah, an idea had traveled two separate roads and arrived intact at both destinations. When a thesis survives independent derivation, it's probably true.

VI · Mid-Cool Phase — The Evidence

What Was in the Cup

Three drinks arrived in competition sequence. I ran each through the only instrument I fully trust — the three-phase thermal method that underpins every entry in my archive. Let the temperature fall. Wait for the truth. Below is the espresso decoded phase by phase — tap through it the way the cup revealed itself.

Crema settled and integrated. Tactile arrives first — a body that coats without weight. Sweetness present from the very first contact.
DECODE: The 1:2.9 ratio's early-phase gift — volatile aromatics from the nitrogen-macerated Gesha ride the heat, while the deep 24% extraction has already pulled the caramelized sugar compounds forward. First sip = tactile evaluation, exactly per rubric.
The lime acidity blooms — bright, lifting, citric-clean. This is where the blend architecture shows itself: florals above, fermented depth below, nothing colliding.
DECODE: Citric and malic acid perception peaks in the mid-cool window as sweetness recedes slightly. The El Diviso Sidra's anaerobic depth stays subordinate — structural bass, not lead vocal. This is what taming the farm looks like.
Clean finish. Nothing lingers bitterly. The cup ends where it should — resolved, complete, no arguments left on the palate.
DECODE: Cool phase is where under-diluted or over-extracted shots confess their sins — chlorogenic acid degradation products turn harsh as temperature falls. This cup had none of that. The generous dilution beyond the bitterness threshold held all the way down. The lean-ratio failures documented across my archive were entirely absent here.

And the other two, in the register they deserve:

He had promised strawberry. Milk drinks are where a concept either survives integration or dissolves into dairy — my archive excludes them from technical audit for exactly this reason: they are a different physics. But judged on its own terms, this one survived. The strawberry note arrived quietly at the back of the throat in the mid-cool window, not as a flavour bomb but as a presence. The coconut lipids softened what the soy proteins would otherwise have made heavy. Real engineering, honestly delivered.
Drink 03 · Signature Beverage
The Signature
LACTO-FERMENTED PASSIONFRUIT · JASMINE SHRUBS · BROWN RICE VINEGAR

Signature drinks are usually where I step back. The genre tends toward spectacle over coherence — ingredients stacked for theatre. And on paper, this recipe should have been my nightmare: lacto-fermentation, vinegar, seven-day funk. Everything my intensity ceiling warns against. Instead, the coffee character stayed visible the entire way through. The floral and elderflower registers of the Iris Estate Gesha carried the top; the fermented passionfruit added tartness without aggression; the jasmine shrubs amplified what was already in the bean rather than overwriting it. Whatever he described, I could taste. In my archive, that sentence is the highest technical compliment that exists.

VII · The Verdict I Owed

What I Said

After the final drink, the other two judges turned to me first. I hadn't expected to lead. But sixty entries of writing honest verdicts into a spreadsheet had prepared me for exactly this moment: when someone hands you a score sheet, you owe them the truth, formatted kindly.

I started where honesty required: the opening concept was excellent. "High Definition" wasn't a clever name bolted onto a routine — it was a load-bearing frame that held the entire fifteen minutes together. His technical depth was obvious. His structure showed months of construction. All of that was true, and it needed saying first, because feedback that opens with criticism gets heard as attack.

Then I told him what I actually observed: when the technical numbers arrived — TDS percentages, extraction yields, ratios — his narrative momentum stalled. He would pause, hunt for the next sentence, and the thread would thin. The numbers were correct. But they weren't yet part of the story.

My prescription was specific: transition phrases. Small verbal bridges — "So where are we now…", "Let me come back to this idea…" — pre-built recovery ramps that let a presenter regain the road without the audience ever seeing the wheels slip. The numbers should arrive as evidence inside a narrative, not as a recitation that interrupts one. Stay in command of the story first; let the data serve it.

On the drinks themselves, I had no material criticism, and I told him why in the exact words of my own archive standard: whatever he described, I tasted. Promise and delivery, matched. That is the entire game.

Ho Ming received all of it the way serious people receive things — leaning in, not bracing. He told me afterward that most people only offer compliments, and what he actually needed was someone willing to name what still needs work. He thanked me for coming across the city and for treating the role seriously.

I told him the truth in return: I have deep respect for what baristas do. Sixty entries of my archive exist because of people like him standing behind counters, trying to transmit a farm's work through a machine and their own two hands. Being asked to help — even one evening, even as a mock judge — was not a small thing to me.

VIII · Cool Phase — Resolution

After the Session

Alex and I left together and shared a taxi toward Botanic Gardens. He was meeting an old colleague named Victor for dinner — and in the kind of coincidence this day seemed determined to keep producing, Victor turned out to be my ex-colleague too. We hadn't seen each other in roughly ten years. A hello, a few minutes of compressed catching-up, and then I left them to their dinner and took the train home.

Home by 9pm. Four coffees deep.

Epilogue · What the Archive Learns

The Entry That Doesn't Fit the Schema

My archive has twenty-two columns. Date, city, café, coffee, varietal, process, dose, yield, ratio, verdict. It is built to hold cups. It has no column for the night the counter turned around — no field for "sat in judgment of a national competitor," no cell for "heard my own founding principle spoken back to me by someone who derived it independently."

But the archive learns things anyway, between the rows. Tonight it learned three:

One. A palate ceiling is not a wall — it is a solo-performance measurement. El Diviso at full volume exceeded me; El Diviso seated in an orchestra scored 71 against my own DNA. Context is a processing method too.

Two. The best ideas in coffee are convergent. When a documenting enthusiast and a competing professional arrive at the same thesis — clarity as the entire point — through completely different roads, the thesis has earned its place at the top of the page.

Three. Sixty entries of honest verdicts, written for no audience but the archive itself, were quietly training me for the moment someone would actually ask for one. The feedback I gave Ho Ming wasn't improvised. It was the sixty-first verdict — just delivered out loud, for the first time, to the person who made the cup.

This Was Worth

Ho Ming steps onto the SNCC stage in five days. The judges there will hold score sheets like the one I held, but their marks will carry consequence — points toward a national title, a seat at the Worlds. I'll be following it the way you follow someone whose work you've now tasted at close range: personally invested, quietly confident, and curious whether the transition phrases made it into the final routine.

Some days are quiet but they carry weight. I cancelled my gym. I crossed the city. I drank the farm that once defeated me and then watched it get orchestrated into something beautiful. I sat in judgment for the first time and discovered the archive had been preparing me for it all along.

Simple dinner tonight. Some TV. Let the day settle at its own pace, the way a good cup does — hot, mid-cool, cool, resolved.

The gym is tomorrow, 10:30am. The archive is forever.

Seven days after this room, Ho Ming walked onto the stage at Sands Expo — and out of sixty-seven competitors, finished second in the nation. I was in the hall on finals day, and by evening the Singapore Coffee Association feed confirmed what the practice room already knew: the routine held, the narrative carried its numbers, and the drinks delivered what they promised. Second of sixty-seven, one place from the Worlds.

And a symmetry this story didn't need but received anyway: the person who opened the door for us that night — Elysia Tan, who turned on the lights of a closed café so two strangers could sit in judgment — won the Brewers Cup that same evening, competing independent, and now carries Singapore's filter colours to the World Brewers Cup. The room we sat in produced a national runner-up. The person holding the door produced a champion.

The sixty-first verdict held up. The archive was right to prepare me.
